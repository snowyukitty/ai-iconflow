## Summary

A storefront whose product promise is "every term of the trade is stated before
you agree" needed a favicon about *looking inside*, not about buying. The winning
mark is the split cloth a shop hangs at its entrance to say it is open, drawn on
a card, with the split forking into the brand initial. It won because it names a
specific object no system icon claims, and because the letter is load-bearing
geometry — the opening itself — rather than a monogram parked on a tile (L22).
The two colours are lifted verbatim from the product's default theme, so the tab
icon and the site are the same object lit the same way.

## What failed first

**Pass 1 — distinctiveness 2.** Three concepts through three lenses: two hanging
cloth panels on a rod; the same with splayed inner edges; and a lantern with rib
lines. At 128px the two-panel version read as the **pi symbol**. A horizontal bar
over two vertical rectangles *is* that glyph, and the 42-unit hem sag drawn to
say "cloth" was invisible below 64px. The lantern read as a **stack of
pancakes** — three dark rib bars across a light oval dominated the shape, and its
outline collided with a bell (L9).

**Pass 2 — distinctiveness 3–4.** Replacing two panels with ONE cloth carrying a
slit cut up from the hem removed the pi reading entirely, because pi requires a
full-height gap. A deliberate control candidate — the same cloth with a plain
vertical slit — then read as a generic **arch or doorway**: legible, unownable.
That control is what proved the fork was carrying the distinctiveness rather than
the object. A full-bleed variant with no card was also tested and rejected: the
light mark on transparent nearly vanished on a white tab bar.

**Pass 3 — final.** The remaining fault was that rod plus cloth read as a
**table**. Thinning the pole from 84 to 80 units while lengthening it from 784 to
872 (overhang 80 → 100 per side) changed the ratio enough to read as a pole a
cloth hangs from. A variant adding a hanging loop between pole and cloth was
rejected under L4: clutter at 128px, mush below 48px.

## Lessons

- [ ] Run the name-the-thing test against **typographic glyphs**, not only
      system icons. L9 covers magnifier / gear / house / bell; a mark can just as
      easily collide with pi, T, H or a plus sign, and a glyph collision is worse
      because the viewer reads it as *text* and stops looking for an object.
- [ ] Put a **control candidate** in the bake-off — the same object without the
      signature device. It is the only cheap way to learn whether the device or
      the object is carrying distinctiveness. Here the plain-slit control scored
      lower and named a different, generic noun, which promoted the fork from
      "nice detail" to "the reason it works".
- [ ] A hanging object needs its support to differ in **ratio**, not just in
      shape. Bar-over-body reads as a table until the bar is long and thin enough
      to read as a pole; the fix was aspect ratio and overhang, not a new
      element.
- [ ] A cut that must read as *split fabric* has to reach the hem. A closed
      interior hole reads as a window or a hole punched in a sign; only a cut
      that opens the bottom edge says the cloth parts for you to walk through.
